# Research methods for AAA

Use the workflows that fit the question. A simple identification can be answered in a sentence with a source; a bibliography or comparison benefits from a table. The evidence fields below are a checklist, not a mandatory output template.

## Eight useful workflows

### 1. Research an artist, writer, curator, or organisation

Start with the supplied name and resolve identity through an actual record. Add the original script, observed romanisations, and documented aliases. Compare roles, dates, associated institutions, and places before merging similar names. A name appearing in a chapter heading, event participant list, correspondence, or archive folder is a different relationship from authorship of the item.

Search both catalogue records and relevant archive folders. For a group or institution, also inspect event organisers and publication publishers: subject keywords alone can miss these relationships. State whether the results concern works, writings, interviews, documentation, or mentions of the person.

### 2. Reconstruct an exhibition or other historical event

Find the event record, then follow its related catalogue and archival materials. Preserve each venue/date pair, touring stops, organisers, curators, and participants as separate facts. Do not assume an event was organised by AAA merely because AAA catalogues it.

Distinguish the event from its catalogue, invitation, preparatory letter, photographs, and later commentary. An earlier letter about an exhibition is not evidence that the exhibition occurred in the letter's year. A listed participant does not establish that a particular work was shown. For reconstruction, seek the catalogue, checklist, installation documentation, or another document that establishes that link.

### 3. Explore a topic, medium, or movement

Break the request into topic, region, period, people, and desired material types. Use `vocabulary.md` to generate a small set of exact, alternative, and related queries. Run narrow searches first; expand one dimension at a time when results are sparse.

Separate evidence about an artistic practice from evidence about the subject depicted or discussed. A keyword describing technology does not necessarily establish a work's medium. A publication about a region does not establish every artist's nationality. Explain material query expansions when they change the scope of the answer.

When the requested period means historical subject coverage, complement material-date filtering with a search without that filter: undated records and later publications can discuss the period. Assess their relevance manually and disclose any exclusion of undated material. Do not assume untested date filters include overlapping ranges.

### 4. Build an annotated bibliography

Verify publication-level details on each record: title, relevant responsibility roles, publisher, edition or volume when supplied, publication date, language, identifiers, and location code. Treat missing or explicitly absent identifiers as missing; do not infer them. Page count describes extent, not the page supporting a claim.

Use chapter headings to locate promising essays, while distinguishing the chapter's author from the publication's editor. Label annotations based only on catalogue descriptions accordingly. Do not imply that a description, cover image, or table of contents is a reading of the whole publication. Keep separate editions and language versions unless the task asks to group them.

### 5. Survey an archive or prepare a research visit

Read the collection overview before counting or interpreting its items. Capture the archive's scope, arrangement, date coverage, languages, provenance, digitisation history, and stated omissions. Preserve collection → series/folder → item relationships when they explain why material is present.

For a visit, produce a prioritised list of relevant records and their access conditions. Use the actual location/reference information supplied by the site. Collection-wide instructions may add context, but an item's restriction still matters. Report application requirements as observed and dated; do not generalise one archive's rules to all holdings.

### 6. Read a primary document, image, interview, or recording

Open the accessible object or attachment after reading its catalogue description. Keep catalogue assertions separate from observations made in the source. For a multi-image record, inspect the relevant images and identify their sequence; one thumbnail is not the whole set. For PDFs, distinguish viewer page numbers from printed pagination.

Preserve uncertainty from difficult handwriting, scanning, transcription, or translation. A transcript is a different evidence form from the recording. Quote only the passage needed and cite its page, image number, or timestamp. If only metadata is available, answer the metadata question and identify the unread material needed for deeper claims.

Attribute retrospective recollections to the speaker and interview date. A remembered event is testimony, not automatically an independently established chronology. For consequential discrepancies, seek contemporary records when available and preserve unresolved disagreement.

### 7. Construct a timeline or compare accounts

Track material creation/publication dates, event dates, archival coverage, and digitisation/publication-on-the-web dates separately. Preserve approximate dates and original precision; a year must not become an invented calendar day.

Compare claims at their proper level: an invitation announces a plan, a review describes an account, and photographs document particular views. Identify disagreements and cite the competing evidence without silently harmonising them. Group multiple records about one event when useful, while retaining their separate provenance. Search failure is not proof that an event, work, or relationship did not exist.

### 8. Connect archival material with research, essays, and programmes

Use interpretive writing and programme pages to find names, references, and research questions, then follow links to the relevant records. Distinguish AAA's own programme from a historical event in the collection's event database. Distinguish an essay's argument from a catalogue description and from a primary source cited by that essay.

When a record points to an external digital edition, verify that destination and label it as an external source. An onsite AAA copy and an accessible edition elsewhere can coexist. Do not describe the external edition as a reading of AAA's physical copy.

For teaching, inspect Learning resources and educator programme downloads. Read the selected guide, identify its audience and language, and connect proposed activities to the original archival records. Distinguish reusable exercises from historical enrolment announcements. Label adaptations as your own; do not attribute an invented lesson to AAA or imply that an old workshop is open for registration.

## Evidence and citation checklist

For substantial research, retain the following where relevant; expose only the fields useful to the user:

- **Identity:** exact displayed title and script; record URL; record kind; archive path or bibliographic identity; identifiers actually supplied.
- **Responsibility:** each person or organisation with the site's stated role. Archive ownership, subject, authorship, documentation, and publication are distinct relationships.
- **Time and place:** the field's meaning, original value, precision, and qualifiers. Preserve material date and event date separately.
- **Evidence read:** metadata, catalogue description, document text, image, transcript, recording, or external edition; precise page/image/timestamp for content claims.
- **Access:** the displayed access label, item-specific notes, and observed ability to open the material. Keep rights statements separate from access status.
- **Provenance of the answer:** live page/document, user-provided copy, or search-index excerpt; date checked; remaining uncertainty.

Prefer a specific item link over a general search page. For a document claim, include the item link for archival context and a page or timestamp; add the attachment link when useful. If a functioning Citation control provides a citation, use it and check its roles and dates against the record. Do not invent an official AAA citation style when the control has not been inspected.

Retain cataloguer-assigned and translated title status where it matters. Do not silently repair suspicious catalogue values, duplicate names, or inconsistent dates: preserve the relevant original and flag the issue. A record's rights holder must not be inferred from its archive owner or hosting institution.
